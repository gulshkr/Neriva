Waking up with sharp shoulder pain when rolling over in bed, struggling to reach into a back pocket, or finding it impossible to fasten clothing—these are hallmark symptoms of **Adhesive Capsulitis**, widely known as **Frozen Shoulder**.

Many patients try to "rest" the arm in a sling or avoid moving it entirely. Unfortunately, with frozen shoulder, complete rest is often the quickest way to turn a temporary inflammatory flare into severe, prolonged joint stiffness that can last over a year.

Here is what is really happening inside your shoulder joint and how structured physiotherapy can safely guide you back to full mobility.

---

## What Actually Causes a Frozen Shoulder?
The shoulder joint is surrounded by a flexible sleeve of connective tissue called the **joint capsule**. 

In frozen shoulder, this capsule becomes inflamed, thickens, and tightens, forming scar tissue adhesions. As the capsule shrinks, the lubricating synovial fluid inside decreases, severely restricting the ball-and-socket motion of the humerus.

Frozen shoulder typically progresses through three distinct phases:
1. **The Freezing Phase (2 to 9 months):** Progressive pain, especially at night, accompanied by gradual loss of movement.
2. **The Frozen Phase (4 to 12 months):** Pain may plateau or lessen, but shoulder stiffness is at its peak. Daily activities become significantly impaired.
3. **The Thawing Phase (6 to 24 months):** Range of motion slowly begins to return, although recovery is often incomplete without guided physical therapy.

---

## Why Rest Alone Fails
Because the capsule is actively contracting and forming adhesions, immobilizing the arm causes the connective tissue fibers to cross-link even more tightly. 

While forceful, aggressive stretching can aggravate the acute inflammatory state, **gentle, progressive, low-load mobility** is essential to signal the capsule to remodel and maintain joint space.

---

## 3 Safe Exercises for Home Mobility

### 1. Pendulum Swings (Codman's Exercises)
- Lean forward with your unaffected arm supported on a sturdy table.
- Let your affected arm hang freely toward the floor.
- Gently use your torso’s momentum (not your shoulder muscles) to swing the arm in small circles, forwards, and backwards.
- **Goal:** Relaxes surrounding muscle guarding and creates gentle passive decompression.

### 2. Finger Wall Walking
- Stand facing a wall about arm's length away.
- Touch the wall at waist level with the fingertips of your affected arm.
- Slowly "walk" your fingers up the wall as high as comfortably possible without shrugging your neck.
- Hold for 5 seconds at the top comfortable point and slowly walk fingers down.

### 3. Towel / Cane Assisted Mobility
- Using a light stick or towel held in both hands, use your strong arm to gently assist the stiff arm through external rotation and abduction.

---

## The Role of Clinical Physiotherapy
A physiotherapist does not just give you exercises—they perform hands-on interventions that you cannot replicate at home:
- **Specific Joint Mobilization (Maitland / Kaltenborn Techniques):** Gliding the humeral head inferiorly and posteriorly to open the tight capsule.
- **Myofascial Release:** Easing compensatory knots in the trapezius, latissimus, and chest wall.
- **Modalities for Pain Relief:** Targeted thermotherapy, ultrasound, and electrotherapy to desensitize the joint before mobility work.
